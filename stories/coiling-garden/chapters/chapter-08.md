# Chapter 8: Undercut

The turning woman was still behind the tins.

Mu-Poe checked every morning now — reached above the stove to the shelf where she'd wrapped it in the cloth from the clay bin and wedged it between the tea caddy and a tin of condensed milk she was saving for emergencies that kept arriving. The cloth was damp from the stove's steam. She touched the form through it — the shoulder, the twist of the spine, the hip where her choices and the serpent's finishing blurred into one surface. Still there. Still warm, though the kitchen was cold enough to see her breath.

She rewrapped it and put it back.

The dog was on his blanket, chin on his paws, watching her with the expression of a creature who had opinions about hiding good work behind tins but lacked the vocabulary to express them.

"Don't start," she said.

Through the window, the road was open. Yesterday's ruts were already softening in the mud — the rain had come in the night, erasing the military's tracks the way rain erased everything, patient and democratic. The bridge was clear. The hills beyond were green and grey and ordinary, as if nothing had crossed them but weather.

She put the kettle on. No tea — she'd used the last of it days ago, before the road closed. Hot water, then. She held the cup between her hands and drank the warmth, which was the point. The drink was secondary.

---

Theo came at ten.

Early. That was the first wrong thing. She heard the shoes on the gravel — that precise, evenly spaced crunch — and knew by the rhythm something had changed. His stride was shorter. Faster. A man walking toward something rather than arriving at it.

He didn't knock. He never knocked — the door was unlocked during working hours, and Theo considered all hours working hours — but today he opened the door with his left hand, which meant his right was occupied, which meant he was carrying something that wasn't his usual case.

An envelope. Cream-colored, official, with a wax seal already broken. He held it the way you hold a verdict — flat, at arm's length, as if the contents might transfer to his fingers.

"Good morning," she said from the kitchen doorway.

He didn't return it. He set his leather case on the hall table, unbuttoned his coat with one hand — less efficient than his usual two-handed method, and therefore significant — and laid the envelope on top of the case.

"The road was closed for three days," he said.

"I noticed."

"I was in town. At the telegraph office. At the municipal records hall. At the Ellmore agency's regional branch, which is a single room above a tobacconist with a filing system I would describe as aspirational." He pulled off his gloves. "I found the name."

She didn't ask which name.

"Petra Vleck. Twenty-six. From Harren, up the coast. The agency placed her at this address on the fourteenth of November." He picked up the envelope. "She never arrived. Because you arrived first."

Mu-Poe leaned against the doorframe. Her hands were in her apron pockets — the left one closed around the broken boiler tooth she'd never taken out.

"The trustees have been informed," he said. "I posted the letter before the bridge closed. It will have arrived by now." He stroked the right needle of his mustache — thinking, not upset. The distinction mattered less than it used to. "They will send someone. An inspector, most likely. Wartime makes these things slower, not less certain."

"What do you want me to do?"

The question surprised him. She could see it in the way his hand paused mid-stroke — a half-second freeze before the needle-point found his fingertip again. He'd expected denial. Deflection. The things she'd given him before.

"I want you to leave," he said. "Before the inspector arrives. Quietly. Whatever you've been doing here — and I have theories, none of them charitable — ends. You pack your things, take your dog, and go. The letter names no individual. If you're gone when they come, there's nothing to investigate but an employment irregularity."

"That's generous."

"That's practical. An arrest creates paperwork. Paperwork creates attention. Attention, in the current climate, creates complications I do not want attached to this estate." He buttoned his coat — slowly, each button a punctuation mark. "I am not a cruel man. I am a precise one. Precision and cruelty are often confused by people who find accuracy uncomfortable."

He picked up his case. At the door — always at the door, never facing her — he stopped.

"One week," he said. "I'll tell the trustees I need additional time to verify. That gives you until Friday."

Another Friday. She almost laughed.

He left. His footsteps down the gravel were measured and even, the same distance between each one, all the way to the gate.

---

She cleaned.

Not because the house needed it — the house always needed it, the way old houses needed, with a patience that outlasted attention. She cleaned because her hands required occupation, and when her hands were occupied her mind could work underneath, the way roots work under pavement — slow, persistent, following the cracks.

She mopped the gallery. The tall windows made their pale underwater light. The sculptures stood in it, patient, untouched by the fact that the woman who cleaned them was about to be expelled from their company. She mopped around their bases and didn't look at the plinth by the window where the turning woman had stood for thirty seconds two mornings ago, fitting into the collection the way a key fits a lock — by being made for it.

She polished brass. She wiped stone. She wound the hall clock, which was twenty-three minutes fast now, the mechanism drifting further without the boiler's rhythm to anchor the house's sense of itself.

In the afternoon she walked the dog. Down the drive, past the gate, along the road toward the bridge. The mud was churned deep — treads or heavy wheels, she couldn't tell which. The ruts held water the color of rust. On the far bank, a farmhouse she'd seen smoke rising from every morning for two months stood dark. No smoke. No curtains in the windows. The door open to the weather.

She stood at the bridge for a long time. The dog sniffed the mud at the roadside.

She walked back. Past the field where the winter crop lay flat from the rain. Past the gatehouse, where Goss was splitting wood in her yard, the axe falling in steady unhurried strokes — a woman who did not adjust her rhythm for the end of the world.

Goss looked up. Nodded. Went back to splitting.

Mu-Poe went inside and sat at the kitchen table with a cup of hot water and the gear tooth in her palm, turning it between her fingers the way she turned clay. Small. Brass. Sheared clean. Evidence of a fracture that had been coming for weeks.

One week.

---

The studio was warm.

Not from the serpent — or not only. Something had changed in the air down here, a deepening she felt as soon as her feet left the last step. The mineral cold was still there beneath, but layered now — cold stone, warm air, the way a room feels when someone has been in it for hours, breathing, existing, filling the space with the simple heat of presence.

She went to the bench. The damp rag was where she'd left it. She uncovered the two pieces — the open palm, the spiral. Felt them both. The palm was still good, the life line holding its shallow crease. The spiral was still rough.

The air gathered. Slowly this time — not the quick arrival of earlier nights but a gradual accumulation, the way dawn comes, not all at once but in degrees, each one so slight you can't point to the moment it started. The warmth moved closer. The coiling against her arms, her neck — the slow spiraling pressure that meant the serpent was near.

The hands found hers. Light. Not tentative — nothing about the serpent was tentative — but careful. The way you touch someone who has pulled away and come back, not knowing yet what the return means.

Mu-Poe stood still and let the hands settle.

She reached for fresh clay. Not the palm, not the spiral — new. She pulled a fist-sized lump from the bin and held it between her palms. The serpent's fingers rested on the backs of her hands, not guiding, not suggesting. Present.

She began to shape. Not a form she planned — not a hand or a figure or a spiral. Something that came from underneath the thinking, from the place where the authorship question lived and burned. She pressed and the serpent's warmth followed. She carved with her thumbnail and the serpent's fingers shifted to give her room. She built the base heavy — the serpent's grammar — and thinned the upper edges — her own — and then found the transition between heavy and thin and stayed there, working the seam where the two impulses met, pressing the clay into a shape that wasn't compromise but conversation.

She didn't know what it was. Her hands did.

The serpent's fingers moved from her hands to the piece — to the other side, the back, working the surface while Mu-Poe built the form. Not the same as the turning woman. Different. The serpent's touch was lighter, less finishing and more — she searched for the word while her hands searched for the shape — more answering. As if Mu-Poe's side of the piece asked a question with every ridge and hollow, and the serpent's side responded.

A conversation. Not the kind that produced a single voice. The kind that let you hear two.

She worked until her hands stiffened with cold. Then she stopped and held the piece — rough still, unfinished, the surface half-smooth and half-raw, the shape ambiguous as a cloud or a word you can't quite remember. But the weight of it was right. The balance between what she'd built and what the serpent had answered sat in her palms like something that had been weighed and found to be exactly what it was. Not enough, not too much. The true mass of the thing.

The serpent's hands covered hers on the piece. Not guiding. Holding. The way you hold something together while the glue sets.

She stood in the dark with the question still in her chest and the clay still in her hands and the serpent's warmth wrapped around her fingers, and the question didn't go away, but it changed shape. Not *whose is this* but *what are we making.*

She wasn't sure those were different questions. But they had different weights, and the second one sat lower, and lower was where things stayed.
