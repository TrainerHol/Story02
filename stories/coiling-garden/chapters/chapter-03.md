# Chapter 3: Blind Contour

The dog got out on the fourth night.

Mu-Poe hadn't left the door open — she was sure of that. She'd latched it the way she always did, the iron catch dropping into the strike plate with a click she checked by habit every evening. But the latch was old and the door had swollen with the damp, and when she came downstairs from winding the hall clock, the back door stood six inches open and the dog was gone.

She called for him from the kitchen step. Once, twice. Her voice went out into the dark garden and came back with nothing.

The grass was wet. She could see his tracks — faint compressions in the overgrown lawn, heading straight for the maze. Not wandering. Straight.

She went back for her shoes and her coat.

---

The maze entrance had once been ten feet wide and twelve feet tall. The growth had narrowed it to a passage barely wider than her shoulders, and the first turn was visible only as a slightly darker darkness ahead.

"Come here," she called. "Come on."

From deep inside, one bark. Then nothing.

She went in.

The first stretch was straight, then branched. She took the left, following the click of his nails on stone — not grass, not soil, but flat pavers set close together, slick with moss, buried under decades of dead leaves. Someone had paved this maze. Or part of it.

Two more turns. She kept her right hand on the hedge wall, fingers trailing along the cold waxy leaves. The old trick for mazes — follow one wall and you'll eventually come out. But she wasn't trying to come out. She was trying to go in.

At a junction the hedge bulged inward — one of the old topiary shapes, grown formless. In the dark, her hand read it as a shoulder. A hip. She pulled back. Just leaves.

The path turned again. She lost the pavers — her feet found bare dirt, packed hard, rutted by something heavier than foot traffic. The dog's panting faded ahead of her. She stopped. The maze was silent except for the drip of water from overhead branches.

She held her breath. There — ahead and below her. Not panting now, but sniffing. The wet investigation of a nose working at something close to the ground.

The hedges closed overhead. At first there had been sky, a pale strip between the tops of the walls. Now the branches wove together and the strip narrowed and disappeared. Just dark. The kind of dark that had weight to it, that pressed against the face like cloth.

The air changed. The green hedge-smell gave way to something underneath it. Mineral. Stone dust. The wet-clay smell from the other nights, stronger now, not carried on the breeze but rising from below.

Her foot found the first step.

Stone. Cut stone, with a squared edge, set into the ground where there should have been nothing but roots and soil. She felt for the next with her toe. Found it. And the next. Going down.

Twelve steps. She counted. Then the ground leveled and the air opened around her — not the tight green channel of the maze but a space. She could feel it in the way sound changed, the way her breathing stopped bouncing back from close walls and softened into distance. A room. Underground.

She stood still and let her hands tell her what her eyes couldn't.

To her left, waist-high: a surface. She touched it. Wood — heavy, scarred, with grooves worn into the grain from years of use. A workbench. Her fingers found objects along its back edge and she picked up the nearest. A chisel. Not the decorative kind from the studio upstairs — this one was worn to the shape of the hand that had held it, the handle dark with oil and sweat, the blade nicked and resharpened so many times the edge had curved. She set it down and found the next. A rasp. A flat file. A mallet with a cracked head bound in wire.

Real tools. Used tools. The kind that had done the work the upstairs studio only pretended to.

On the corner of the bench her fingers found a small depression in the wood — a mark, repeated. She traced it. Two curved lines, nested, like parentheses or a seed. Mable's mark. She'd felt it on the base of every sculpture in the gallery while cleaning, carved so small you had to look for it. Her thumb knew this shape.

She moved along the bench. A bin of clay, cool and damp, with a cloth laid over it to keep it workable. Next to it, stone dust fine as flour — marble dust, the same dust she cleaned from the gallery floors every morning. Her fingers closed around a fistful of the clay and held it. Dense, cool, with that particular yielding resistance she knew from years of handling it in borrowed studios and rented rooms. Better than anything she'd been able to afford. She made herself let go and wiped her hand on her coat.

Past the bench, on a stand: a shape. She reached for it carefully, both hands, the way you reach for something in the dark that might be fragile or might be alive. Her palms found a figure — a woman's torso, half-emerged from rough stone, arms at her sides, head tilted back. Unfinished. The front was polished to that impossible smoothness she recognized from the sculptures upstairs, the same skin-that-forgot-warmth. The back was raw stone, not yet shaped. A work in progress that would never be finished.

She held it for a long time.

The dog was at her feet. She heard him settling — the small grunt and sigh, the turn, the nose tuck. Not distressed. Content, the way he was content by the stove. As if he'd found the place he'd been pointing toward for four nights and could finally rest.

The air smelled like turpentine and wet clay and something older — sealed stone, undisturbed earth, the deep mineral cold of a place that hadn't known light in years. Maybe ever.

This is where she worked, Mu-Poe thought. Not upstairs with the decorative chisels and the burlap-wrapped marble. Here. In the dark.

She had matches in her coat pocket. A box she carried from the kitchen out of habit — two left, maybe three. She'd been meaning to move them to the drawer.

She struck one.

Sulfur flared. A bright spike of yellow that hurt after so long in the dark. She blinked and held the match up.

Nothing.

Bare earth. Exposed roots, thick as wrists, arching through a shallow cavity where the topsoil had washed from beneath the maze's root structure. A depression in the ground the size of a small room — natural, unremarkable. No bench. No tools. No bins of clay or sculpture on a stand. Just dirt and roots and stones and the dog, lying on the ground, looking up at her with mild disappointment.

The match burned to her fingers. She dropped it.

Dark. And the bench was there under her left hand — the scarred wood, the worn chisel exactly where she'd set it down, the bin of cool clay, the half-finished figure on its stand. All of it. Solid. Present. The stone dust gritty beneath her shoes.

She stood very still.

She took out another match and held it between her thumb and finger for a long time. Then she put it back in the box.

She sat down on the stone floor and leaned against the workbench. The dog shifted and pressed his ribs against her thigh. She put her hand on his side and felt him breathing — slow now, steady, the heartbeat that had been a compass needle for four nights finally at rest.

Down here: a bench. A chisel. A block of clay. A dog. Her hands.

She stayed.
