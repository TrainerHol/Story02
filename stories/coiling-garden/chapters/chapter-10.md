# Chapter 10: Greenware

She broke the brick tea with the back of a knife.

The compressed leaf crumbled dark across the cutting board — dried, smoky, foreign. She'd been saving it since Wednesday, though saving it for what she hadn't decided. Friday morning in a house with no heat and a deadline seemed like the sort of occasion that didn't need a reason.

The kettle steamed. She tipped the fragments into the pot — too much, probably, but this wasn't a morning for economy. The water hit the leaf and the kitchen filled with a smell from somewhere far away, somewhere the war was distant weather and tea was not a thing you broke with the back of a knife.

The dog watched from his blanket. Both ears up — full attention, not the usual one-eared morning kind. He tracked her movements with the studied focus of a creature who understood routines and had noticed this one was wrong.

"It's tea," she said.

He did not look reassured.

She poured. The tea was dark and tasted like smoke and earth and the kind of decisions you've already made — flat, committed, no longer requiring heat. She held the cup between her palms. The warmth entered her hands the way the serpent's warmth entered clay: all the way through, into the grain.

She hadn't packed.

Through the window, the hills were grey shapes under a sky the color of old plaster. She drank her tea and waited for the sound of shoes on gravel.

---

He came at noon.

Late for Theo — a man whose punctuality was a point of professional identity didn't arrive late without the lateness meaning something. She heard the gate's iron complaint first, then the gravel, his stride shortened and quickened. A man arriving at something he'd hoped would be finished.

She was in the front hall. Not waiting. She'd moved the reaching woman two inches left on her plinth because the light from the west window caught the wrist better at that angle, and she'd been thinking about it for weeks, and today felt like a day for doing things she'd been thinking about.

The door opened.

Theo looked at her standing among the sculptures and his face went through three stages in the time it took him to set his case down: surprise, disciplined; anger, stored; and something she hadn't seen before — the recalculation that happens when a prediction fails and the model has to be rebuilt.

"You're here," he said.

"I'm here."

He unbuttoned his coat slowly, each button punctuation. Hung it on the hook by the door — the hook she'd oiled in December, which received his coat with well-maintained silence.

"The terms were clear."

"They were."

He walked past her to the reading table and set his case down. Opened it. Inside: the ledgers, the measuring tape wound tight. He didn't take anything out. He stood with his hands on the open case and his back to her, and the right needle of his mustache was lower than the left, which meant he was thinking about something he didn't want to say yet.

"I have been in correspondence," he said. "Or attempting to. The postal service between this district and the capital is running at a delay of ten to fourteen days. The trustees' administrative office was relocated in January — the forwarding address is a postal box that may or may not be staffed. The inspectorate has been reassigned to wartime credential verification, which is a polite term for checking papers at border crossings rather than investigating employment irregularities at country estates."

He turned.

"The inspector is not coming," she said.

"The inspector is delayed." His hand found the mustache — the left needle, a correction-stroke. "Delay is not the same as cancellation. But the distinction has become academic."

"How academic?"

"Sufficiently academic that I have stopped scheduling my mornings around it." He closed the case. Both latches — click, click. "Which leaves us here. You, in a house you have no right to occupy. Me, cataloging a collection in a district that may not be under our government's jurisdiction by March."

"Then why are you still here?"

The mustache stopped. Both needles frozen — the right mid-stroke, the left at rest. She'd never seen them still at the same time.

He looked at the gallery. The reaching woman with her impossible wrists. The bust on the far wall. The wave-and-hands in the corner. His eyes moved between them with something that wasn't appraisal — something less guarded. A man looking at objects he'd spent months reducing to numbers and seeing something his system could not contain.

"Because this is the finest private collection of sculpture produced in this century," he said. Quietly. Not performing. "And I am the only person keeping a record that it exists."

He picked up his coat.

At the door — always at the door, always in profile — he stopped.

"I know you're not a maid," he said. "I've known since your second week. A maid doesn't handle marble the way you do — palm flat, reading the grain. That's not cleaning."

He pulled on his gloves, one finger at a time, and left.

His footsteps on the gravel were measured, but the spacing was wider — each step carrying more weight, or more thought, or whatever it is that changes a man's stride when the ground has shifted.

---

She stood at the window with her cold tea and drank it because the cup needed emptying and her hands needed occupation.

*Palm flat. Reading the grain.*

She'd thought she'd been careful. She hadn't. You could hide a name but you couldn't hide the way your hands knew stone. Three months of cleaning Mable's sculptures — every surface touched, every edge learned — and her fingers had told Theo what her mouth never would. He'd watched. Theo watched everything. And he'd seen the thing she couldn't disguise: the difference between a woman cleaning a sculpture and a woman studying one.

She washed the cup. Put it in the cupboard.

At two she walked the dog. The road was quiet — no convoys, no carts. Goss's gatehouse was closed, the yard swept, the woodpile gone inside. The chickens worked their enclosure with the focused industry of creatures whose economy required no institutional support.

At the bridge she stopped. The river was high from rain upstream — the water rust-colored, carrying things. A branch. A plank. Something that might have been a gate, tumbling slowly in the current. The debris of places already finished with the process the estate was only beginning.

Luden's farmhouse. Door open. No crow. The stain on the floorboards would be wider now.

She walked back. Past the field. Past the gatehouse. The dog walked at her heel — not trained to, but choosing to, the way animals choose proximity when the air changes.

---

The studio was warm.

Not the warmth of recent nights but something deeper — the stone itself holding heat, as if the ground had been breathing warm for hours. She felt it through her boots on the first step.

The hands found hers on the fourth step. Not the bottom — halfway down. She stopped. The warmth closed over her fingers and held.

She sat down on the step.

The stone was warm under her. The serpent's warmth gathered against her hands, her arms, crept up to her shoulders, settled against her back through her coat. She leaned into it the way you lean into something that has always been there and you've only just stopped pretending you don't need.

No clay tonight. No tools. No piece on the bench waiting for attention. She was in the studio and the work was not the point. The hands on hers were the point. The warmth against her spine was the point. Two presences in the same dark, doing nothing except being there.

The serpent's hands moved over hers — slowly, without direction. Not mapping, not reading. Something simpler. The way you hold someone's hand when there's nothing left to say and the holding is the sentence.

She thought of Theo standing in the gallery with his voice gone quiet. *The finest private collection of sculpture produced in this century.* She thought of Mable, who had stood here. Thirty years of this dark, this warmth, these hands. Mable had never told anyone.

The understanding arrived the way the serpent's corrections always arrived — not as a thought but as a weight, pressing up from underneath. Mable hadn't told because there was nothing to tell. Not because the secret was dangerous — it was — but because the telling would require a language that didn't exist. How do you explain a love conducted entirely in darkness, in clay, in the pressure of hands you've never seen? What words fit? The words weren't made for it. Mable's silence wasn't secrecy. It was accuracy. The only honest thing.

Mu-Poe sat on the fourth step with the serpent's warmth wrapped around her and the silence that was Mable's only true legacy settling over her like something inherited.

After a while — an hour, or more; the studio's time was its own — she stood. The hands released her slowly. The warmth drew back — not gone but withdrawn, the way a tide withdraws. Still near. Still there. But giving back the shore.

She climbed the steps. The maze was black. The sky was low cloud, no stars. The frost had settled on the hedges and the air smelled of cold stone and winter earth.

She went inside. The dog was at the back door. She sat on the kitchen floor and held the gear tooth between her fingers and the silence between her ribs.

Friday had passed. She was still here. The next thing would be the next thing.
