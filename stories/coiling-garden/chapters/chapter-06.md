# Chapter 6: Wedging

Friday morning, and the radiator didn't fire at all.

She lay in the narrow bed in the servants' quarters and listened for it — the clank at six, or five-forty, or five-thirty-two, depending on the boiler's declining relationship with punctuality. Nothing. The house was silent in a way that felt deliberate, as if the building were holding its breath along with her.

She got up. Washed her face. The water was cold — the pipes hadn't warmed overnight, which meant the boiler had quit entirely, not just slipped. She dressed and went downstairs in the dark, her feet finding the treads by habit.

The kitchen was grey. Dawn arrived tired, a slow leaking of light that turned the room from black to the color of old newsprint. The dog was on his blanket by the stove, which had gone out. He lifted his head when she came in, one ear up, and put it back down.

She rebuilt the fire. Split the last of the kindling — she'd need to break up the crate from the scullery if no coal came today — and nursed the flames until the stove ticked with heat. Tea. The last of the loose-leaf, measured with the care of someone rationing ammunition.

Through the window, the maze was edged with frost. Beyond it, the hills matched the sky — pewter on pewter.

She sat with her tea and waited for the sound of Theo's shoes on the gravel.

---

He didn't come at eleven.

She'd positioned herself in the front hall — not waiting, she told herself, just polishing the brass barometer that hadn't worked since before she arrived. The barometer was mounted by the door and gave her a clear view of the drive through the sidelight window. She polished the casing, the glass, the dial with its frozen needle. Nobody came.

At noon she moved to the library. The left vine-handle had started to stick — cold working into the hinges the way cold works into everything given time. She took apart the mechanism, six screws, a spring plate, a hinge pin the size of a matchstick, and cleaned each piece with machine oil from the gardener's shed. Reassembled it. The door swung free. She oiled the right one too, because her hands needed occupation.

Nobody came.

At one she ate — the turnip Goss had left, boiled with salt, and a scrape of dripping on bread. She gave the dog the turnip water. She washed the plate and put it in the cupboard and stood at the sink with her hands on the basin's edge, her eyes on the drive.

Empty gravel. No leather case, no measuring tape, no mustache.

She'd expected something. A confrontation. A letter hand-delivered by someone with a stamp and an expression of institutional regret. *By Friday,* he'd said. Friday was half over and the house was as quiet as the sculptures in it.

That was worse. A threat you could see, you could brace against. An absent one just floated, settling into every room like the dust she cleaned each morning — into the creases first.

She went down to the boiler.

The timing mechanism was frozen. Not slipped — seized. A gear tooth had sheared off, the shale coal finally winning its slow argument with the metalwork. The broken tooth sat in the bottom of the housing like a pulled molar, brass, sheared clean. A stress fracture ran through its base. This had been coming for weeks.

She couldn't fix it. Not without a replacement gear, and the engineer was east of the river, and the machinist in town had closed up last month and gone somewhere safer.

She put the broken tooth in her apron pocket and went upstairs.

---

Goss was at the service step when she opened the back door — not leaving eggs but standing there, one hand raised to knock. She was a tall woman, rawboned, with hands larger than Mu-Poe's and a face that looked like it had been carved by someone who didn't believe in ornament.

"Road's blocked," Goss said.

"Which road?"

"The only road." She jerked her chin south. "Military requisitioned the bridge for transport. Nothing in or out until they're done."

"How long?"

Goss shrugged — a single efficient motion, nothing wasted. "Day. Two. They don't tell you." She looked past Mu-Poe into the kitchen. "Your boiler sounds wrong."

"Gear tooth broke."

"Mm." She reached into her coat pocket and produced two eggs wrapped in a square of cloth. "Baker's shut. His boy came back from the east." She set the eggs on the step. "They shipped him home in a box."

Neither of them said anything for a moment. Wind moved across the frost-stiff grass between the house and the gatehouse, carrying the smell of cold earth.

"Bad teeth on a boiler mean bad coal," Goss said. "Next delivery, tell them no shale."

"If there is a next delivery."

Goss looked at her directly — the first time in seven weeks, straight on, without the sidelong gatehouse glance. "There'll be a delivery. Or there won't. Either way you'll need wood."

She turned and walked back across the lawn. Her boots crunched the frost in a heavy, deliberate rhythm — the footsteps of a woman who didn't alter her pace for weather.

Mu-Poe picked up the eggs. Through the garden wall, she could see the road below — a barrier across it, soldiers in clusters, steam-trucks lined up waiting. The bridge was hidden by the curve of the hill, but she could hear the low rumble of heavy vehicles crossing, one after another, patient and mechanical.

Theo was on the wrong side of that bridge. His letters couldn't go out. His proof sat in a leather case in a town she couldn't get to and he couldn't leave.

She allowed herself one breath that wasn't held.

---

She waited until the light bled out of the sky and the frost resettled on the lawn. The dog watched her put on her coat — one ear folded, one up, knowing.

"Stay," she said.

He stayed.

The maze took her in. She moved through it without hands on the walls now, her body turning at the turns the way a body turns in a familiar hallway at night — by the knowledge that lives below thought. The pavers were slick with ice. The hedges crackled when her shoulder caught them.

Twelve steps down. The mineral cold. The dark that had weight.

She went to the clay bin and pulled back the cloth. The open palm she'd been working on was there under a damp rag. She uncovered it and ran her fingers over the surface. Still good. The life line she'd carved with the serpent's guidance was holding its shape — shallow, earned, the kind of crease that came from use rather than cutting.

The air didn't thicken.

She waited. Stood with her hands on the clay, breathing. The same dark, the same mineral smell, the same cold stone under her feet. But the warmth that gathered when the serpent arrived — the slow pooling, the pressure, the coiling — didn't come. The studio felt larger without it. Emptier, the air thin and still where it usually turned.

She worked alone.

It was different. Not wrong — she could sculpt, she knew clay, her hands remembered what they'd been doing since she was fifteen. She shaped the fingers of the open palm, curling the index and middle finger inward, working the knuckles from inside the form the way you always work knuckles — by pressing outward, letting the bone find the skin. She thinned the wrist, because her instinct was always to thin, to refine, to make the clay lighter than the earth it came from. Then she caught herself. Pressed the clay back. Kept the weight low, the way the serpent had taught her.

Taught. That was the word, and the word was the problem.

She was sculpting from memory — not her own memory of clay but her memory of the serpent's corrections. Following instructions that weren't being given. Making one finger shorter than the others because that's what the serpent preferred. Keeping the base heavy because that's what the serpent wanted. Every choice she made she could trace to a pressure on her wrist, a guided thumb, a warmth that meant *yes* or a cooling that meant *not that way.*

She stopped. Set the piece down. Pressed her palms flat on the bench.

She picked up fresh clay. Rolled it between her hands and let the shape come on its own. Not a hand, not a figure — a spiral. A coil, thickening at the center, thinning at the edges, the clay turning in on itself the way a fern curls before it opens. She worked it for an hour. The proportions weren't right. The surface was the surface of a student's work — competent enough to show the idea, rough enough to show the distance between idea and execution.

It was hers.

She set it on the bench beside the open palm and felt the difference with both hands. One was better. One was hers. She didn't know yet if those could be the same thing, and the not-knowing sat in her chest the way the broken gear tooth sat in her apron pocket — small, sharp, evidence of a fracture that had been coming for a while.

The air shifted. A thickening at the edges of the room — slight, like someone stepping into a doorway without entering. Not close. Not touching. But there. The temperature rose a degree, maybe two.

Mu-Poe kept her hands on the spiral and didn't reach out.

"I needed to know," she said. To the dark. To the warmth at the edge of it.

The warmth held. Neither closer nor further. Patient, the way the bench was patient, the way the clay was patient, the way anything that existed only in darkness had learned to be.

She stayed until her fingers stiffened with cold. Then she covered both pieces — the palm and the spiral — with the damp rag and climbed the twelve steps into the maze, into the frost, into a Friday that had passed without the sky falling.

The dog was asleep by the dead stove. She didn't relight it. She sat on the kitchen floor beside him and pressed her back against the wall and felt the cold of the house settle around her like something she'd chosen.

Her hands smelled like clay and earth and nothing else.

She closed her eyes.
