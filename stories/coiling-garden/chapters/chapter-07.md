# Chapter 7: Casting

Three days the road stayed closed.

She checked the road from the garden wall each morning. The barrier was still up. The bridge still closed. Nothing in or out.

The house got colder. Without the boiler, the radiators were dead iron — she knocked the one on the second-floor landing and it rang hollow, the sound of a bell with no tongue. She kept the kitchen stove alive by breaking up a crate from the scullery, then a shelf from the empty pantry, then two garden stakes she pulled from the frozen ground out back. The wood was green and smoked badly. The kitchen smelled like a campfire built indoors, which is what it was.

Goss left a bundle of split firewood on the service step, tied with baling twine. No note. Mu-Poe left a polished brass hinge plate she'd found in a drawer in the butler's pantry — heavy, shaped like an oak leaf, too beautiful for its purpose, which described most things in this house. Whether Goss needed a hinge plate was beside the point. The exchange continued because exchanges were what kept you tethered when everything else simplified toward survival.

She cleaned. She mopped. She oiled brass and wiped stone and maintained the estate the way her father had maintained walls — not because anyone asked, but because stopping meant something was over, and she wasn't ready for that.

Every night she went down.

---

The serpent came back by degrees. First the air thickening, on the second night after Mu-Poe's test. Then the warmth, closer than before — not pushed but given up, the way a distance closes between two people who've stopped pretending it's comfortable. Then the fingers, settling over hers when her hands were already in the clay. Lighter than before. Less guiding. More listening.

Something had changed. She felt it in the quality of the touch — not correction anymore, not the confident hand of a teacher repositioning a student's grip. Something more tentative. The serpent was paying attention to what Mu-Poe chose, and the attention had weight.

She was making a woman turning. Not a full figure — a torso, the body caught mid-rotation, one shoulder forward and one pulling back. The twist was the problem. Getting the tension between forward and back so the clay looked like it was moving and being held still at the same time. A moment caught. The kind of form you could circle and never find the same pose twice.

The serpent's hands followed but didn't lead. Mu-Poe pressed clay along the spine, shaping the ridge of vertebrae under skin, and the warmth deepened. She thinned the waist — her instinct, always — and the warmth held. She'd expected the press of resistance, the serpent pushing the clay back toward thickness. It didn't come.

She found a line between the serpent's weight and her own impulse to refine. A middle grammar she hadn't known she was looking for. The torso narrowed at the waist and widened at the hips and the transition was neither heavy nor light but the thing between, which was hers.

They worked for hours. Four hands on one piece, two visible to no one, two visible to nothing. Mu-Poe built the structure — bones beneath form, tension in the twist, the specific angle of a shoulder blade pressing outward from inside the way shoulder blades do when the body turns away from something it still wants to face. The serpent finished surfaces. That warmth passed through the clay and left it smoother than tools could manage, as if the material had been persuaded to forget it was earth.

At some point the hands were no longer on hers. They were on the piece itself, working the other side while Mu-Poe worked this one. She could feel the clay shifting under a pressure she wasn't applying. Two sets of intentions, and the intentions agreed — not because one followed the other, but because they'd arrived at the same place from different directions.

When it was finished — when her hands circled the piece and found nothing that needed adding and nothing she wanted to take away — the serpent's warmth pressed against her back. Both hands, not on hers, not on the clay. On her. Between the shoulder blades, then lower — palms flat against her ribs, fingers spread, holding her the way you hold something you've been making and have realized is complete.

She stood still. The air coiled against her arms, her neck — slow, spiraling, warmth that had nothing to do with clay. The presence behind her was tall. She could feel it above her head, around her shoulders, an architecture of heat that mapped a body she would never see. The hands on her ribs pressed closer. Through her coat, through her shirt, the heat reached her skin the way sunlight arrives through glass — not quite touching, but felt all the way through.

She put her hands over the serpent's and held them there.

They stood like that, the finished piece cooling on the bench beside them, and for a long time neither of them moved, and neither of them needed to.

---

She wrapped the piece in the cloth from the clay bin and carried it up the twelve steps. Heavy — denser than she expected, as if the serpent's finishing had added something to the material that wasn't weight but acted like it. Conviction.

The maze was grey with pre-dawn mist. Hedges dripped on her shoulders. She walked the turns with the piece held against her chest, her chin resting on the wrapped form, boots finding pavers by memory.

The kitchen was dark. The stove had gone out. The dog lifted his head from his blanket, one ear up, and watched her set the shape on the table with the focused attention of a creature who understood that some things his person carried mattered more than others.

She unwrapped it.

Dawn came through the window the way it had been coming all winter — slowly, grudging, the light resenting another cold day. It reached the table and touched the clay.

The woman was turning. One shoulder forward, one pulling back, the spine a line of contained motion that Mu-Poe's hands had known in the dark and her eyes were meeting for the first time. The proportions were hers — the asymmetry, the weight settled low but not heavy, the waist that belonged to neither the serpent's grammar nor her own but to the language they'd built between them. She could point to her choices. *Here, this angle, that ridge, the way the hip drops.*

The surface was not hers.

She ran her hand along the back. Smooth. Not the impossible smoothness of Mable's gallery pieces — not skin-that-forgot-warmth — but close. Close enough to see the family resemblance. The serpent's finishing was in the grain, in the way the clay caught morning light without reflecting it, in the warmth that persisted in the material hours after the hands had lifted. Not invisible. Not dominant. Present the way a second voice is present in a harmony — you could hear it if you listened, and once you heard it you could never again be sure the melody was anyone's alone.

The face was deliberately vague — she'd left it that way, because the piece was about the body's motion, not the identity. But the jaw was specific. Narrow, set at an angle she recognized from the bust on the second-floor landing, one of Mable's nameless women. She hadn't copied it. But her hands had been educated on Mable's surfaces for two months of cleaning and studying, and the serpent's guidance carried echoes of the same teacher, and in the finished piece three hands had written one sentence and she couldn't find which words were whose.

---

She carried it to the main gallery.

Not to display it. She just needed to see.

The tall windows cast the same pale directionless light that made the sculptures look like they floated. She set the turning woman on the empty plinth by the window — the one Theo hadn't assigned a number to, because even his system ran out of certainties eventually. She stepped back.

It belonged.

Not the way a student's work belongs in a class exhibition — tolerated, contextual, clearly less. Not the way a forgery belongs — too perfect, the effort of imitation showing in the polish. It belonged the way a sentence belongs in a paragraph. Same voice. Same rhythm. Same hand. Different words, maybe, but the same mouth shaping them.

She looked from the turning woman to the bust across the room, the one with the jaw she hadn't meant to echo. Then to the reaching woman with the impossibly thin wrists. Then to the abstract in the corner — the wave-and-hands that was also a mouth, one of Theo's phantom two.

The quality was the same. The surface was the same. The signature — not Mable's carved mark but the serpent's invisible one, the warmth pressed into the grain of the material — was the same.

She thought of the rough spiral in the studio below. That honest, clumsy coil she'd made alone. The distance between the spiral and this turning woman was the distance between what Mu-Poe could do with her own hands and what she could do with the serpent's, and the distance was Mable Rilutz's entire career.

She took it off the plinth.

---

She wrapped it and set it on the shelf above the stove, behind the tins, where nobody would find it unless they knew to look. She washed her hands — clay under every nail, the warmth between her shoulder blades persistent as a question she couldn't stop answering wrong.

The dog pressed against her leg. She scratched behind the folded ear and looked through the window.

The road below was empty. The barrier was gone. The bridge was clear — she could see the far bank, the road continuing east into the hills, open. Whatever had closed it had moved on in the night, and the landscape looked like nothing had happened except for the ruts in the mud.

The road was open. Theo could come today.

She put the kettle on and stood at the window with her back to the shelf where the turning woman sat wrapped and hidden, and waited for the sound of shoes on gravel.
