# Chapter 12: Seam

The cold woke her.

Not the cold itself — she'd gotten used to that, the way you get used to a house that breathes winter through its walls after the boiler dies. What woke her was the dog, pressing against her ankle on the floor, his nose pushing at her hand with the focused insistence of a creature who had decided it was time to be up.

She dressed and went down. The stove needed stoking — she fed it kindling from the crate pieces and nursed the flame. The dog was already up, sitting by the back door with his ears working independently, one forward and one swiveled east, monitoring two directions at once.

The conversation piece sat on the windowsill where she'd left it. Morning light touched both surfaces — the smooth half holding warmth, the rough half casting its small thumbnail shadows. A day and a half it had been there. Anyone walking past the window could see it. No one had walked past the window.

She put the kettle on. Brick tea — she was rationing the block now, shaving pieces with the knife, each morning slightly less than the one before. The smoke-and-distance smell filled the kitchen but thinner, stretched, the last of something being made to last.

Through the window, the sky was the color of old tin. Low. The hills were lost in it — the horizon gone, the world contracted to the estate and the fields around it and the road that led away from both.

---

He came at eleven.

The usual approach — gate, gravel, measured stride. She heard him in the front hall: the case set down, the coat on its hook. The gallery door. His shoes on the parquet, each step a measurement of the space between plinths. She washed her cup and listened.

Twenty minutes. Thirty. The sound of a ledger laid open on the reading table. The measuring tape — a brief metallic purr as he extended it, the click as he locked the length. Forty minutes. Then silence — the particular silence of a man writing, the scratch of nib on paper audible only because the house was so quiet.

Then footsteps. Not toward the front door. Toward the back of the house. Toward the kitchen.

He appeared in the doorway — the first time he'd stood there, the first time he'd crossed the line between the gallery's pale underwater light and the kitchen's woodsmoke warmth. He was in his waistcoat, the coat left on its hook, which meant he was staying.

"The gallery is nine degrees," he said. "I measured with a thermometer I carry for assessing storage conditions. Nine degrees is below the threshold at which I can write legibly." He looked at the stove. "This room is warmer."

"It is."

"I need to complete the secondary measurements for the west-wall grouping. May I use your table?"

She nodded. He went back to the gallery for his case, returned, set it on the kitchen table. Opened it. Ledger, measuring tape, the small brass thermometer, three pencils sharpened to identical points. He arranged them with the precision of surgical instruments and sat down.

Then he saw it.

His eyes had been on his instruments, on the table, on the process of establishing a workspace in unfamiliar territory. When he looked up — the habitual scan of a man who needed to know where things were — his gaze went to the window, to the sill, to the piece.

He didn't react. Not immediately. She watched the stages: the eyes resting, the recognition forming, the mustache going still. Both needles. The full stop she'd seen twice before.

He stood. Crossed to the window. Didn't pick it up — not yet. He looked at it on the sill the way he looked at pieces in the gallery: from a distance first, the overall form, the proportions, the way it held the room.

Then he picked it up.

His hands were different from hers. Long fingers, maintained nails, the hands of a man who handled valuable objects for a living — careful, supporting the base, letting the form speak before his fingers did.

He turned it. The smooth half caught the kitchen light and held it — that warm receptive quality, the grain accepting light rather than returning it. His thumb found the surface. He ran it along the curve the way she'd seen him run the measuring tape along gallery pieces — slowly, reading.

His thumb crossed the seam.

He stopped. The thumb sat on the line where smooth became rough, where the serpent's surface gave way to Mu-Poe's. He pressed. Felt the transition — the density change, the temperature shift, the moment where one kind of attention became another.

He turned the piece and found the rough half. His thumb traveled the scored ridges, the uneven pressure, the deliberate imperfection. He held the piece in both hands now — the smooth half in his left palm, the rough in his right — and stood at the window with the grey light on it and the look of a man whose measurements had just encountered something they couldn't contain.

"The finishing on this surface" — his left thumb on the smooth half — "is consistent with the Rilutz collection. Twenty-three pieces in the main gallery share this quality. The surface density. The way the grain receives light." His voice was quiet. Not performing. "I have attributed it to Rilutz's technique. It is the signature I have been cataloging for three months."

He turned the piece.

"This surface" — the right thumb on the rough half — "is different. The score marks are deliberate. The weight is intuitive — the base is lighter than a formally trained sculptor would build it."

His hand went to the mustache. The right needle. A long slow stroke.

"And the seam between them is visible. Which is not a failure." He set the piece back on the sill. Carefully, the way you set down something that has changed the room it's in. "It is a statement."

He looked at her.

"You made half of this."

Not a question. A reading. The same precision he applied to everything — the ledgers, the mustache, the measurements that added up to a career built on seeing what was in front of him and describing it accurately.

"Yes," she said.

He waited. The mustache moved — one stroke, two, shorter arcs, faster than his thinking-rhythm. A man running calculations that didn't produce a number he recognized.

"The other half," he said. "The half that matches the collection." He paused. "I would like to understand how a self-taught sculptor working in a dead woman's kitchen is producing surfaces that are indistinguishable from the work of Mable Rilutz."

Mu-Poe leaned against the counter. The gear tooth in her apron pocket pressed against her hip. She didn't answer. Not because she was hiding — because the answer didn't fit inside the question. The same silence Mable had kept for thirty years. The only accurate thing.

Theo waited. His hand on the mustache — one stroke, two. A man calculating how to catalog a silence.

He sat back down at the table. Opened his ledger. His hand was steady — the nine-degree cramp cured by the kitchen's warmth and the presence of something that defied his inventory.

He worked. She worked — sweeping, wiping the counter, stoking the stove, the maintenance of a house that required attention regardless of what sat on its windowsill. They occupied the kitchen for two hours without speaking. The piece between them on the sill, both surfaces legible, the seam running through it like a sentence neither of them could finish. The dog slept on his blanket. The light moved and the shadows of the piece's rough half moved with it and Theo wrote in his ledger without looking up, and Mu-Poe cleaned without looking at the ledger, and the kitchen held them both the way rooms hold people who have run out of categories for each other.

At one he packed his instruments. Closed the case — click, click. Stood.

At the kitchen doorway — not the front door this time — he stopped.

"I would like to come back tomorrow," he said. "To work in this room. The temperature is preferable."

"All right."

He nodded. A single precise motion. He went to the front hall, collected his coat, and left. His footsteps on the gravel carried the even spacing of a man who had added something to his catalog that didn't have a column yet.

---

At two she walked the dog.

Past the gate, down the road. The ruts were dry today, the mud hardened into ridges that caught her boots. Goss's gatehouse had smoke from the chimney — the door open, Goss visible inside doing something with her hands, the focused silhouette of a woman who maintained things because maintaining was what you did.

The dog walked at her heel. Not trained to — choosing to. The air was still and cold and smelled of frost and the fields beyond the wall.

She didn't go far. To the bend in the road where you could see the bridge, and back. The estate behind her when she turned, the hedge maze dark against the grey sky, the house with its chimneys and its cold gallery and its kitchen where a man had crossed a threshold that morning and found something his ledger couldn't hold.

---

The studio was warm.

She went down at midnight with clay under her nails from no work and Theo's words still sitting in her chest — *you made half of this* — the way a sentence sits in a body after it's spoken. Not heard but held.

The serpent's hands found hers at the bottom of the steps and drew her to the bench. Clay. Fresh. Pulled from the bin and set on the surface — the serpent offering, not directing. *Here. The material. Begin.*

She took the clay. Held it. The cool dense potential of it — the fact that it could be anything and was currently nothing. She pressed her thumbs into the center and began to open the form. Not a figure. Not a hand, not a spiral. Something else. She didn't know what yet. Her hands would find it when they were ready, and until then the clay was clay and the dark was dark and the gap on the shelf was waiting.

The serpent's fingers settled on the clay beside hers. Not guiding, not following. Parallel. Two voices beginning a sentence neither of them knew the end of.

She worked until the cold stiffened her fingers. Then she covered the piece — half-formed, shapeless still, the first gesture of something that would take nights — and climbed the steps.

The maze smelled of frost and earth and the cold green of hedges in winter. The clouds were low. The night was quiet — the kind of quiet that old estates hold around themselves like a coat, muffling everything beyond the walls.

She went inside. The dog. The floor. The gear tooth.

The conversation piece on the windowsill caught the first grey suggestion of dawn. Both surfaces. The seam between them. The seam that Theo's thumb had crossed and that his ledger could not contain.

She closed her eyes. The dark behind her lids held the weight of everything she knew and couldn't say, and the shape of something new begun in the dark below.
