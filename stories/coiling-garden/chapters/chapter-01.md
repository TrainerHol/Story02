# Chapter 1: The Grain of It

The dust on Mable Rilutz's sculptures collected differently than ordinary dust. Mu-Poe had noticed this in her first week. On the bureau in the front hall, on the windowsills, on the clockwork radiator that clanked and hissed every morning at six — dust settled the way you'd expect, in a fine democratic film. But on the sculptures, it gathered in the creases first. The fold of a knuckle. The groove beneath a collarbone. As if the dust were trying to learn the shapes by feel.

She wiped the bust on the second-floor landing with a cotton rag, working the cloth into the channel between lip and chin. The face was a woman she didn't recognize — no plaque, no title card, just a head and shoulders emerging from rough stone like someone half-drowned in granite. The lips were slightly parted. The expression was the kind of calm that came after something terrible, not before.

Mu-Poe folded the rag to a clean edge and started on the eyes.

She could have been faster. A maid was supposed to wipe and move on. Dust the surfaces, mop the floors, keep the brass from greening. That was the job. You cleaned things. You didn't study them.

But her hands had never been good at being fast when they could be careful instead. Her father had laid brick the same way — slower than the other men on the crew, but his walls didn't crack. Her mother's stitching was tight enough to hold water. Slowness was the family trade.

She traced the line of the jaw with her thumb. Not cleaning now. Just feeling.

The stone was cool and impossibly smooth — smoother than marble had any right to be, with a grain so fine it felt like skin that had forgotten how to be warm. She'd touched enough stone to know the difference between carved and something else. Chiseled marble had a texture beneath the polish, a fibrous roughness, like the memory of the tool that shaped it. This had none. The surface was continuous. As if it had been grown, not cut.

She pulled her hand back and wiped the bust with the rag.

Downstairs, the clockwork radiator clanked twice and went silent. That meant seven. The boiler's timing gear had slipped again — it ran twelve minutes fast since the last coal delivery had been half shale, and the engineer who used to service it was somewhere east of the river now, with every other man under forty.

---

The kitchen still had most of what she needed. Tea, if she didn't mind it weak. A quarter-loaf she'd stretched to thirds with dripping. Two eggs from the neighbor's hens — the neighbor, a woman named Goss who lived alone in the gatehouse and raised chickens with a kind of furious competence, had taken to leaving them on the service step without a word. Mu-Poe left a cleaned pair of candlesticks in return last week. They hadn't discussed it.

The dog — a dun-colored mutt with one ear that folded and one that didn't — sat by the stove and watched her eat. He'd been hers for three years, longer than any address. She gave him the heel of the bread.

Through the kitchen window, the garden was grey and overgrown. Beyond it, the hedge maze rose in walls of dark green, twelve feet high in places, the topiary shapes at the corners long since grown into shapeless bulges. Past the maze, the hills rolled south toward the river, and past the river, if you listened at night with the windows open, you could sometimes hear the guns. Not close. Not yet. A low rhythmic thumping, like someone knocking on a door very far away.

Mu-Poe washed her plate, dried it, and set it in the cupboard beside the three others she used. The rest — a full service for twenty, painted with foxglove and fern — stayed in their felt-lined drawers. She picked up the mop and went back to work.

---

The main gallery took most of the morning. Thirty-two sculptures spread across three connected rooms, each lit by tall windows that let in the kind of pale, directionless light that made everything look slightly underwater. Some were figures — women, mostly, in poses that weren't quite natural. One reached upward with both arms, fingers spread, the stone of her wrists so thin it looked like it should snap. Another sat with her hands in her lap, her face turned away, her spine curved in a way that made Mu-Poe's own back ache in sympathy.

Others weren't figures at all. Shapes that coiled and folded, surfaces that seemed to shift depending on where you stood. A piece in the corner of the second room looked like a wave breaking from one angle and a pair of hands clasped in prayer from another. She'd walked past it four times before she noticed it also looked like a mouth.

She mopped around their bases and tried not to look too long.

Mable Rilutz had been dead for eleven months. The newspapers — the ones that still printed, between the paper shortages and the requisitions — had called her the greatest sculptor of the century. Some said of any century. She'd worked in this house for thirty years, producing pieces that galleries fought over, that collectors mortgaged estates to own, that critics described with words like *transcendent* and *impossible* and, once, memorably, *as if the stone had been waiting to become this and she merely permitted it*.

Mu-Poe had seen a photograph of her once, in an art journal she'd stolen from a dentist's waiting room at fourteen. A tall woman, white-haired, standing beside a work-in-progress with a chisel in one hand and an expression of absolute focus. The kind of focus that looked like anger if you didn't know better.

She wrung the mop and moved to the third room.

---

Theo Thregee arrived at eleven, as he did every Tuesday and Thursday, with his leather case and his measuring tape and his mustache.

The mustache deserved its own mention. It was waxed to points so fine they looked structural — two dark needles extending past the edges of his face, maintained with a devotion that Mu-Poe suspected involved small tools and a magnifying mirror. It was the kind of mustache that entered a room before the man did and lingered after he'd gone. She had once watched him eat a boiled egg without disturbing it, which she considered a feat of engineering that bordered on the sacred.

"Good morning," he said, setting his case on the hall table and unbuttoning his coat with the practiced efficiency of a man who cataloged unbuttoning.

"Morning."

He produced cotton gloves from his breast pocket and pulled them on with a faint snap. "I'll be in the east gallery today. Pieces thirty-one through forty." He consulted a small leather notebook. "Also, I'll need to remeasure the torso in the library. My calipers may have been off by a millimeter."

"I'll stay out of your way."

"Much appreciated." He paused, adjusting a cuff. His eyes moved across the hall — across the bust on the landing, the coatrack, the clockwork barometer by the door that hadn't worked in months — and landed briefly on Mu-Poe. "You've been here how long now?"

"Six weeks." Which was true.

"Mm." He stroked the left needle of his mustache with his index finger. "The previous arrangements for the house — were they through the Ellmore agency as well?"

"I wouldn't know about previous arrangements."

"No," he said. "I suppose you wouldn't."

He picked up his case and walked toward the east gallery, his footsteps precise on the tile, each one exactly the same distance from the last. Mu-Poe watched him go. Then she picked up her bucket and went the other way.

---

The afternoon was quiet. She polished the brass fittings on the library doors — handles shaped like coiled vines that had gone green at the edges — and cleaned the windows on the south side, where the glass was old enough to warp the view. Through the wavering pane, a zeppelin drifted east, low and slow, its riveted belly catching the pale winter sun. A military craft — she could tell by the gun platforms slung beneath the gondola, black against the silver envelope. It passed over the maze, over the hills, and disappeared toward the river.

She cleaned until the light failed, which came early now — four o'clock, the sun behind clouds that didn't so much move as thicken. She lit the gas lamp in the kitchen, gave the dog the last of the dripping on a crust, and sat at the table with a cup of weak tea, her hands wrapped around the warmth.

The house settled around her. Creaks and ticks. The radiator's last wheeze before it gave up for the night. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked twice, an old sound she'd stopped flinching at.

She looked at her hands. There was marble dust under her thumbnail. She didn't wash it off.

Outside, the hedge maze was a dark mass against the darker sky. The dog padded to the back door and stood there, nose to the gap at the bottom, sniffing at something she couldn't smell.

"Not tonight," she told him.

He looked at her, one ear up, one ear folded, and didn't move.
