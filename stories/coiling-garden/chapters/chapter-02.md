# Chapter 2: Rough Work

She'd been putting off the east wing. Not by decision — she just kept finding other rooms that needed her first. The library brasswork. The third-floor bedrooms where mice had gotten into the drapes. The scullery, where steam had warped a cabinet door so badly it took her an hour with a plane and a damp cloth to coax it shut.

But today was east wing day, because Theo Thregee had said he needed the main gallery clear until noon, and Mu-Poe had learned that the simplest way to avoid Theo was to be somewhere he wasn't.

The east wing held Mable's studio — not the gallery but the working rooms behind it. Two doors, both unlocked. The first room was neat. Clean. A worktable stood against the wall beneath tall windows, bare except for a set of chisels arranged by size and a magnifying glass on a brass stand. A block of uncut marble sat on a low platform in the center, still wrapped in burlap. No dust on it — someone had covered it recently, or the burlap was new.

The room was wrong and Mu-Poe knew it immediately.

A sculptor's studio should be a mess. Stone dust in the cracks. Fragments swept into corners. Rags stiff with plaster. Water buckets gone cloudy. Her father's brick sites had more creative disorder than this. A working space accumulated evidence — you couldn't shape stone without leaving traces of the shaping.

This room had none. The chisels looked unused. The magnifying glass was decorative. The marble block was stage dressing — a studio for someone who wanted visitors to believe she worked here.

She picked up one of the chisels — the smallest, a quarter-inch flat — and held it the way you hold a chisel, with the shaft resting against the heel of her palm and her thumb along the side. The balance was good. The edge was sharp. She turned it in the light and saw no wear marks on the blade, no stone residue in the grain of the handle. Brand new, or never used. She set it back in its place, exactly where it had been, matching the faint outline it had pressed into the felt lining of the table.

She tried the second room. Smaller, windowless. A storage space — shelves lined with sealed boxes, a stack of canvas drop-cloths in the corner, a broken easel leaning against the wall.

But behind the drop-cloths, pushed against the back wall where it clearly wasn't meant to be found, was a sculpture.

Small — eighteen inches, maybe. A woman kneeling, arms wrapped around her own ribs, head down. The pose was unremarkable. What caught Mu-Poe was the surface. She crouched and ran her fingers along the shoulder, down the arm, across the knuckles.

Rough. Tool marks still visible in the stone. The proportions slightly off — one arm longer than the other, the angle of the neck not quite right, the fingers thick where they should have thinned. It was the kind of work you'd find in a student's first year. Competent enough to show promise. Raw enough to show how far the promise had to travel.

Mu-Poe sat on her heels and looked at it.

She had a piece like this at home — at the room she'd rented before the estate, locked and paid through the month with money she didn't have for next month. A kneeling figure, different pose, same quality. The same rough honesty. The kind of work that said: *I know what I want to make. I just can't make it yet.*

She covered the sculpture with the drop-cloth and left the room.

---

Theo arrived at eleven with a problem.

"Forty-nine," he said, setting his case down harder than usual. The left needle of his mustache dipped slightly, which Mu-Poe had come to recognize as agitation.

"Pardon?"

"Forty-nine pieces in the main gallery. My initial survey recorded forty-seven. The estate inventory filed with the trustees lists forty-seven. The insurance documentation — which I have verified against three separate appraisals — lists forty-seven." He pulled off his gloves finger by finger. "There are forty-nine."

Mu-Poe leaned the mop against the wall. "Maybe you miscounted the first time."

The look he gave her could have etched glass. "I do not miscount."

She believed him. A man who ate boiled eggs without disturbing his mustache did not miscount.

"Two pieces are unaccounted for," he continued, opening his notebook to a page bristling with colored tabs. "One in the second gallery — the abstract in the corner, the wave-and-hands. I have no provenance record for it whatsoever. The other is a bust in the north hallway that does not appear in any catalogue, any photograph, any review." He snapped the notebook shut. "It is as though they materialized."

"Maybe she made them at the end. Before she died."

"Mable Rilutz had not produced a new work in eighteen months before her death. The trustees confirmed this. Her dealer confirmed this. No new marble was delivered." He stroked his mustache — right needle this time, which meant thinking rather than upset. "And yet. Two extraordinary pieces. Both utterly without record."

He looked at her with the expression of a man arranging facts into a pattern he didn't like.

"You clean these rooms daily," he said.

"I do."

"Have you noticed anything? Stone dust where it shouldn't be? Anything moved?"

She thought of the too-clean studio. The rough little sculpture behind the drop-cloths. "No," she said. "Just dust."

"Mm." He picked up his case. At the gallery door he paused without turning. "The agency that placed you here — Ellmore and Marsh. I telephoned them yesterday."

Her hands stayed still on the mop handle.

"They have no record of a placement at this address in the past six months," he said. "Administrative error, no doubt."

He walked into the gallery. Mu-Poe picked up her bucket and went the other way.

---

After lunch — half a tin of potted meat she split with the dog — she went outside to take in the washing. The line ran from the kitchen door to a post by the garden wall.

A convoy was stopped on the road below the estate. Three steam-trucks with canvas backs, their boilers ticking as they cooled. Soldiers sat on the tailgates, young men in wool coats too thin for the weather, eating from tins with their fingers. One had taken his boots off and was examining his feet with the focused attention of someone who'd been walking for a long time.

A corporal walked up the drive. He was maybe twenty, with a face that looked recently promoted from boyhood.

"Ma'am. We're heading east. Could we trouble you for water? Engine's overheating."

"There's a pump by the stable."

"Much obliged." He looked past her at the house — the tall windows, the brass fittings, the sheer impractical size of it. "This the Rilutz place?"

"It is."

"My mother had a print of one of her sculptures. On the parlor wall." He scratched his jaw. "The Weeping Woman, I think."

"I don't know the names."

He nodded, already losing interest, and walked back to his men. She stood at the garden wall and watched the convoy grind back to life. Steam hissed from the lead truck's patched boiler. The soldiers climbed in, the bootless one last, hopping on one foot while he laced up. They pulled onto the road and headed east, toward the river, toward the sound that came at night — that low, patient thumping she'd stopped thinking of as thunder weeks ago.

Their boot prints stayed in the mud of the drive for days.

---

The dog spent the evening at the back door again.

He'd always been easy — a stove-side creature, content with crusts and proximity. Three years she'd had him, through four rooms in three towns, and he'd never cared about anything past the threshold. But something had changed. Three nights running now he'd pressed himself against the door from sundown, nose to the gap, one ear cocked toward the garden.

Toward the maze.

"You're not going out," she told him.

He looked at her with the expression of a dog who heard the words and was choosing to process them at his own pace.

She washed the dishes. Dried them. Wound the hall clock — brass gears behind glass, beautiful and twelve minutes wrong. She sat at the kitchen table with tea she'd steeped too long and read three pages of a water-stained novel she'd found in the servants' quarters about a woman who inherited a house, which Mu-Poe found funny in a way the author hadn't intended.

The dog whined. Low, sustained, the kind of sound he made during thunderstorms.

She put the book down and looked at the window. The maze was a dark mass against the sky, shapeless where it had once been sculpted. No moon. The hedges were twelve feet of living wall that no one had trimmed since the gardener was conscripted.

She thought she smelled something. Not the green smell of hedgerow — something mineral. Wet clay, or stone dust. The kind of smell that shouldn't carry on garden air.

Then it was gone, and it was just the night again.

She latched the back door, picked up the dog — he weighed more than he looked — and carried him to the stove. She sat on the kitchen floor with him in her lap and felt his heartbeat against her palm, fast and steady, pointed at the door like a compass needle.

Her hands smelled like turpentine. She hadn't touched turpentine today.

She sat there, the dog warm against her ribs, the house ticking around her, the war thumping somewhere past the river, and the maze outside the window — dark, overgrown, and patient.
