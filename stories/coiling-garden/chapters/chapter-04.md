# Chapter 4: Armature

She didn't sleep. She sat on the kitchen floor with the dog until the radiator clanked at six, then she stood, washed her face in cold water from the tap, and started the day as if nothing under the ground had changed anything above it.

It had, of course. The sculptures knew.

That was foolish — they were stone, they didn't know anything. But when she dusted the bust on the second-floor landing, her hands moved differently over it. Yesterday this face had been a mystery of craft — how did Mable get the surface so smooth, what tools, what technique. Today it was an answer. The answer was: not up here. Not with the decorative chisels and the felt-lined table. Down there, in the dark, where the bench was scarred and the clay was cool and the air smelled like turpentine and sealed earth. Where things existed only when you couldn't see them.

She wiped the dust from the fold beneath the chin and moved on. But her thumb lingered on the stone a beat too long, feeling for the grain that wasn't there.

---

The morning was cold and the boiler was worse. The timing gear had slipped again — it ran twenty minutes fast now, which meant the radiators fired at five-forty and quit by noon. Mu-Poe spent half an hour in the basement with a wrench and a rag, trying to coax the escapement back to something approaching regularity. The mechanism was beautiful under the grime — brass gears meshing in a sequence she could almost follow, each tooth engaging the next with a precision that reminded her, uncomfortably, of the too-perfect surface of the sculptures upstairs.

She got it to eighteen minutes fast. Close enough.

Goss had left two eggs on the service step again, and a turnip the size of a fist. No note. Mu-Poe left a polished doorknob she'd found in a box of spare hardware — brass, heavy, with a patina that came up gold under a cloth. Whether Goss wanted a doorknob was beside the point. It was the exchange that mattered. You left things. Things appeared. Nobody discussed it.

Through the kitchen window, the sky was the color of old pewter. The hills were grey shapes under it, patient, waiting for spring or whatever came first.

---

Theo came at eleven.

He was different today. He set his case down quietly. He did not mention his mustache's measurements or his calipers or the discrepancy between forty-seven and forty-nine. Instead, he stood in the front hall with his coat still buttoned and looked at Mu-Poe the way a man looks at a lock he's already picked but hasn't opened yet.

"I made some inquiries," he said.

She wrung the cloth she was holding into the bucket. Gray water. "About the sculptures?"

"About you."

She wrung the cloth again, though it was already dry.

"The Ellmore agency confirmed — they placed no one at this address. The position was filled independently, they said, by someone who contacted the estate trustees directly with credentials that, upon review, appear to have been" — he paused, selecting the word with the care of a man choosing a chisel — "manufactured."

"That sounds like their problem."

"It sounds like yours." He unbuttoned his coat. Slowly. Each button a small declaration. "The trustees have authorized me to verify the employment records of all current staff. There is one current staff member. That is you." He folded the coat over his arm. "I'll need your papers. Employment letter, references, identification. By Friday."

"I'll look for them."

"Do."

He walked past her toward the gallery. At the door, he stopped. Not turning — that wasn't how Theo delivered his best lines. He delivered them to the middle distance, as if the information were too important for direct eye contact.

"The trustees are patient people," he said. "I am not."

He went in. The door closed behind him with the soft, definitive click of a man who had practiced closing doors.

Mu-Poe stood in the hall with the bucket and the wrung-out cloth and the knowledge that she had until Friday to produce papers that didn't exist for a person she wasn't.

She cleaned the windows. She mopped the gallery after Theo left. She polished the brass vine-handles on the library doors until her reflection warped in them, distorted and coppery, a version of herself she didn't recognize. The house was the same as yesterday — the ticking clock, the wheezing radiator, the pale light through old glass. But she moved through it like someone walking through a room they'd already decided to leave.

---

She waited until full dark.

Not for caution — Theo was long gone, and Goss's gatehouse showed no light past nine. She waited because she wanted to be sure this was a choice, not an impulse. The dog running into the maze had been accident. Following him had been reflex. But going back — alone, deliberately, with no excuse and no dog pulling her forward — that was something else.

She left the dog inside. He stood at the back door as she slipped through it, one ear up, one folded, watching her go toward the thing he'd been pointing at for five nights. She didn't look back.

The maze was easier the second time. Her feet found the pavers under the leaves, and her hand found the hedge wall, and the turns came in the right order without her having to think. Left, left, right, past the topiary bulge that felt like a shoulder. The hedges closed overhead. The sky disappeared. The green smell gave way to mineral.

Twelve steps down.

The studio opened around her like a held breath releasing. She stood at the bottom and let the dark settle. There — the bench, the tools, the clay bin. The half-finished torso on its stand. All present. All waiting, the way a room waits when someone has just stepped out.

She went to the clay bin and pulled back the cloth. The clay was cool and dense under her fingers. She took a fistful, then another, and set them on the bench. She didn't have a plan — she hadn't sculpted with fresh clay in months, and even then it had been on a borrowed table in a rented room with light and a mirror and her own eyes to guide her. This was different. This was her hands and the dark and whatever the dark contained.

She began to shape. A simple form — a closed fist, because fists were forgiving. The thumb first, establishing the fold. Then the knuckles, pressing the clay with the heel of her palm to find the right tension between open and closed. She worked slowly, the way her family worked — brick by brick, stitch by stitch. No rushing. Just the clay and the quiet and the smell of wet earth.

Her hands were warm. The clay was cool. She found the rhythm of it — press, turn, smooth, press — and the fist began to emerge from the lump the way fists do, not all at once but in stages, each pass revealing a little more of what was already inside the material.

Then the air changed.

Not a sound. Not a smell. A pressure — a thickening, the way the atmosphere shifts before a storm, except localized. Close. Behind her first, then around her, then — she held still — against her hands.

Warmth. Not the warmth of the clay warming under her palms. A different warmth, with weight and intention behind it. Fingers. She felt fingers settle over hers — long, precise, cool at the tips and warm at the base. They didn't grip. They rested. Two hands laid over her own, following the shape of her knuckles, fitting into the spaces between her fingers as if they'd been measured for the purpose.

She stopped breathing.

The hands didn't move. They waited — patient, present, no more threatening than a second pair of gloves. The air coiled. She felt it against her forearms, a slow spiraling current, like something large and sinuous turning in place very close to where she stood. The warmth deepened. The clay beneath her palms softened — not melting, but yielding differently, responding to a pressure she wasn't applying.

She breathed.

The hands moved. Gently — a redirection more than a correction. Her right thumb had been pressing too hard on the knuckle ridge, flattening what should have curved. The fingers over hers guided the pressure sideways, easing the clay into a shape she'd been reaching for and missing. The difference was immediate. Under her palm, the fist became more itself — the tension between the closed fingers sharpening, the knuckles rounding into the specific tightness of a hand holding something it wouldn't let go of.

She followed the guidance. Turned the piece. The hands turned with her, adjusting, suggesting. Not taking over — there was no sense of being pushed aside, of her hands becoming instruments for someone else's vision. It was more like — and she wouldn't have this thought until later, climbing the twelve steps back to the surface with clay under her nails and her heart loud in her throat — it was more like a conversation. Two people speaking the same language at different levels of fluency, and the more fluent one leaning in, not to correct, but to help the sentence find its end.

She worked for an hour. Maybe two. Time moved differently in the dark, without light to mark its passing. The hands stayed. Sometimes they guided. Sometimes they simply rested on hers, present, warm. Once, when she paused to flex her fingers, the warmth shifted — not the hands but the air around them, a brief pulse of heat against her wrists that felt, unmistakably, like encouragement.

When she finally stopped, the fist on the bench was the best thing she'd ever made. She couldn't see it. She could feel it. Every surface answered her fingers with the rightness of a form that had arrived where it was going.

She stood there with her hands on the clay, the other hands still resting on hers, the coiling warmth against her arms. She should have been afraid. She was in a studio that didn't exist in the light, underground, in the dark, with something she couldn't see touching her. By every reasonable measure, this was the moment to strike a match, climb the steps, and never come back.

She lifted one hand from the clay. The fingers over it stayed, then slowly withdrew — not pulling away but releasing, the way you release a bird you've been holding, opening your palm and waiting.

She set her hand back on the clay.

She stayed.
