# Chapter 11: Bisque

She took them from behind the tins at first light.

The kitchen was grey — the stove banked, the dog still on his blanket, the brick tea smell fading into the house's baseline of old wood and cold stone. She set both pieces on the table side by side. The turning woman and the conversation piece. The cloth came off and she stood over them with her hands at her sides and let the morning do what morning did to things made in the dark.

The turning woman held the light the way the serpent's clay always held light — not reflecting but receiving, taking it in, the grain of the surface warm in a cold room. The twist of the torso, the contained motion, the shoulder pulling back while the body moved forward. Extraordinary. Anyone who saw it would know. Three hands had written one sentence and she still couldn't find which words were whose.

The conversation piece sat beside it the way a rough stone sits beside a polished one — present, unbothered, not competing. The smooth half caught the light the same way — the serpent's surface, warm, receptive. But the rough half scattered it. Kept some, gave some back. The ridges where her thumbnails had scored the clay cast small shadows that moved with the morning. The seam between the two halves ran through the piece like a breath between voices — one side sure, one side present, and the transition neither hidden nor displayed.

She touched the turning woman. Smooth under her palm. Warm still — or warm again — that residual heat the serpent's finishing pressed into the material, as if the clay remembered the hands that had shaped it. Then the conversation piece. Her side. The roughness under her fingertips, the small imperfections that were evidence of her own choosing.

She picked up the turning woman. Heavy, dense, that conviction the serpent's finishing added to the material. She wrapped it in the cloth from the clay bin.

She picked up the conversation piece. Lighter. The rough side grainier to hold, less persuaded, more honest. She carried it to the kitchen window — east-facing, where the morning came in grey and thin — and set it on the sill.

Not behind the tins. Not wrapped. Not hidden.

The light touched both surfaces. The smooth half held its interior warmth. The rough half held its shadows. Together they sat on the sill the way the piece itself existed — two things making one shape, neither pretending to be the other.

The dog lifted his head. Looked at the windowsill. Looked at her. Put his head back down with the expression of a creature who had witnessed a decision and was prepared to accept its consequences without understanding them.

---

She walked the dog at ten. Past the gate, down the road. The military ruts had dried into ridges that caught her boots. No traffic. The bridge was clear. The river had dropped, leaving a tide-mark of debris along the far bank — branches, mud, a scrap of canvas caught in the reeds. The wreckage of places upstream already done with the process the estate was only beginning.

They walked back. Past Goss's gatehouse — smoke from the chimney, the first in days. The smell of burning wood and something cooking. Evidence of a woman inside, maintaining, persisting. The chickens scratched in their wire. The door was closed. The world went on in the places where people decided it would.

---

She carried the turning woman down at midnight.

Through the back door, across the frost-stiff garden, into the maze. The hedges were black shapes against a sky that was slightly less black. She moved through the turns by body memory, the wrapped piece held against her chest, her chin resting on the cloth the way it had the first time she'd carried it — but in the other direction now. Up then. Down now. Taking it home.

Twelve steps. The mineral cold gave way to warmth on the fourth step. The dark thickened. She felt the serpent before she reached the bottom — the coiling pressure, the temperature rising around her arms, her shoulders. The warmth pressed close. She hadn't come down carrying something before, and the serpent's attention gathered around the shape in her arms the way heat gathers around a new object in a warm room — finding the edges, learning the mass.

She went to the bench. Set the turning woman on the surface. Her hands found the edges of the cloth and pulled them back — uncovering the piece by feel, the twist of the torso, the shoulder, the hip. She ran her fingers over the surface one last time. The serpent's finishing under her palm. The warmth still in the grain.

She stepped back.

The air shifted. The warmth moved — not through her but around her, flowing past and gathering over the bench. The serpent was touching the turning woman. Mu-Poe felt it in the displacement of heat, the way the room's temperature reorganized itself into a single point of attention. The serpent reading its own surface. Its own finishing, returned.

Then the warmth moved away from the bench. A displacement of heat tracking across the studio — past the tool shelf, past the clay bin, toward a part of the room Mu-Poe had never gone because the bench was where the work was and the work was the reason she came.

She followed.

Her hands found a wall she hadn't touched before. Stone, rough, colder than the bench side. Her fingers trailed along it and met a ledge — a shelf cut into the rock, waist-height, deep enough to hold a form. And on the shelf: a shape.

She reached. Her fingers found clay — a surface finished with the same impossible smoothness, the same warmth in the grain. A figure. Small. A seated woman with hands in her lap, the posture of someone waiting. The surface was the serpent's, but the structure beneath — she pressed gently, feeling the proportions, the weight — was heavier than Mu-Poe's work. Thicker at the base, broader. A different grammar. Mable's grammar, but less confident. Earlier. A first conversation between sculptor and serpent, the language still being learned. The clay was cold — finished long ago, the serpent's warmth faded from everything except the surface memory.

She moved along the shelf. Another piece. Larger. A hand — an open palm, fingers slightly curled, the life line carved deep. She knew that life line. She'd carved her own version of it, guided by the serpent, weeks ago. But this one was older, deeper, the channel worn smooth by years. The craftsmanship was better — the proportions more balanced, the weight distribution more assured, the seam between sculptor and serpent invisible. Mable years into the collaboration. The grammar refined. The partnership grown past its rough edges into something fluent.

Another. A torso, abstract, the form caught between body and wave. She recognized the gesture — the wave-and-hands in the gallery upstairs. Theo's phantom piece, one of the works that didn't add up in his ledgers. This was its twin, or its draft, or its truth. The version that stayed in the dark while the finished one went up into the light.

More shapes further along the shelf, fading into the dark. She didn't touch them all. She didn't need to. The progression was legible — Mable's work moving from tentative to fluent to seamless, the seam between sculptor and serpent closing piece by piece over decades until it disappeared entirely. The same arc Mu-Poe had felt in her own hands, compressed. The distance between the seated figure and the abstract torso was Mable Rilutz's entire career, held on a shelf in the dark, never cataloged, never measured, never reduced to a number in a leather-bound ledger.

The warmth returned. The serpent's hands found hers and guided them along the shelf — past the torso, past two more shapes she didn't stop to read — to the end. To where the turning woman now sat. Among the others. In the progression. Given its place.

Mu-Poe stood with her hand on the turning woman — her piece, their piece, the collaboration that had belonged in the gallery upstairs but belonged here more — and felt the weight of what she was standing in. Not a studio. An archive. The record of everything Mable and the serpent had made together, kept where it was made, where the only way to know the work was by touch.

The serpent's hands settled over hers on the shelf. Warm. Not guiding, not finishing. The way you let someone stand in a room they've earned.

She stood there. The shapes on the shelf extended in both directions — behind her, Mable's early uncertainty; ahead, the turning woman's hybrid voice. And somewhere in the arc between them, a gap. Not empty — waiting. The place where whatever came next would go.

---

She climbed the twelve steps and came out into a maze that smelled of frost and earth and the cold green of winter hedges.

The kitchen was dark. The dog pressed against her leg at the door. She let him in and stood at the window.

The conversation piece sat on the sill in thin moonlight. Rough side and smooth side, the seam between them legible to anyone who looked through the glass.

She pressed her palms flat on the windowsill, on either side of the piece. The rough surface cold under her left hand. The smooth surface warm under her right. She stood with the honest work between her palms and thirty years of hidden work beneath her feet, and the cold came in through the window that didn't quite close.

The dog settled on his blanket. One ear up. Watching her, or watching the piece, or watching whatever it is dogs watch when the air carries something they can't name but know to be wary of.

She closed her eyes. The dark behind her lids smelled like clay and cold stone and distance.
