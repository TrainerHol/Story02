# Chapter 9: Slip

She walked to town on Wednesday because the tea had been gone for days and hot water was starting to taste like giving up.

The road was soft with rain, the military ruts holding rust-colored water that reflected nothing. Past the bridge, the farmhouse on the far bank stood with its door still open. Nobody had come to close it. Rain had been getting in — she could see the darkened floorboards from the road, the stain spreading inward from the threshold the way stains do, patient, following gravity. A crow sat on the chimney and didn't move when she passed.

Town was half a mile south and half of what it had been two months ago. The butcher's was shuttered — boards nailed flat across the window, not the temporary kind. The tobacconist had a CLOSED sign, hand-lettered, nailed permanent. Above it, the single room where the Ellmore agency kept its regional filing system was dark. The hardware shop was locked. The baker had been gone since his son came home in a box.

The grocer was open. A woman behind the counter with shoulders that had stopped trying.

"Tea," Mu-Poe said.

"Brick tea. Compressed. Last of it." A dark block the size of a fist, dense, smelling of smoke and somewhere far away. "Two marks."

Mu-Poe paid. The woman wrapped it in newspaper three weeks old — headlines about a front line that had moved since printing.

"Most people up the hill have left," the woman said. Not asking. Reporting. "The Drennens, Tuesday. The family with the twins, last week. All west."

"I saw Luden's place."

"Luden buried his wife in September and didn't have reason to stay after that." She wiped the counter with a rag that was already clean. "My husband says the guns are echoes off the hills. My husband's never been right about distances."

Mu-Poe walked back with the tea brick and a tin of sardines and the knowledge that the safe part of the map was pulling back like a tide, and the estate sat close to the waterline.

---

Goss was in her yard, moving firewood inside. Not splitting — moving. Armload after armload through the gatehouse door, out of sight. The chickens scratched behind their wire, occupied with economies that didn't include artillery.

"Need a hand?" Mu-Poe said from the wall.

Goss shook her head. One motion. She set down an armload and straightened, her large hands hanging at her sides the way hands hang when they've worked since before dawn and don't need to perform anything between tasks.

"You'll want yours inside too," she said.

Mu-Poe looked at her own woodpile against the kitchen wall — crate pieces, garden stakes, the shelf from the pantry. Exposed. Wet from last night's rain.

"How bad?"

Goss picked up another armload. At the doorway she paused — half in profile, her rawboned face giving away nothing it hadn't decided to give.

"Bad enough to prepare," she said. "Not bad enough to run."

She went inside. The door closed. The chickens kept working.

---

She cleaned. Not because the house needed it — the house would need it long after she was gone, the way old houses needed, with a patience that outdated any particular set of hands. She cleaned because she had two days and her body didn't know how to wait without working.

She swept the gallery. The tall windows made their usual underwater light. The sculptures stood in it — the reaching woman, the seated figure, the wave-and-hands in the corner. She swept around their bases with the slow care of someone memorizing a room by the labor of maintaining it. The broom moved. The dust gathered. The figures watched or didn't.

*Whatever you've been doing here — and I have theories, none of them charitable — ends.*

She'd been cleaning. That's what she'd been doing here. And learning. And lying. And going down twelve steps every night to put her hands on clay and feel another pair of hands close over them and make the work better than she could make it alone and make the question of whose work it was impossible to answer and make the leaving — when it came — mean something more than losing a job she'd never been hired for.

She swept the dust into the pan and carried it to the kitchen and stood at the window with a cup of hot water — not the brick tea yet, she was saving it, though saving it for what she couldn't say — and watched the empty road and the grey hills and the sky that looked like it had stopped trying to decide what kind of weather to be.

---

The studio was warm.

Not the ambient warmth of recent nights but something deeper — a heat in the stone itself, as if the ground were breathing and the breath was warm. She felt it through her boots on the first step, then in her palms when she touched the wall going down. By the twelfth step the air had weight. The dark pressed close, not empty but full — the way a room is full when someone has been in it for hours, filling the space with the simple fact of existing.

The serpent was there before she reached the bench.

Not gathering slowly, not arriving in degrees. Already present — the warmth against her arms, her neck, the slow spiraling pressure immediate, as if the serpent had been waiting at the foot of the steps the way the dog waited at the back door. Pointed at the arrival. Certain of it.

The hands found hers and guided them to the piece — the conversation, still half-finished on the bench under its damp cloth. Mu-Poe uncovered it and set her palms on the rough surface of her side. The serpent's fingers moved to the smooth half and began to work.

The touch was different tonight.

Not lighter, not heavier. More deliberate. Each pass of the serpent's hands pressed deeper into the clay, compressing the grain, making the surface denser. Mu-Poe felt it through the form — the vibration of pressure traveling from the serpent's side to hers, the material tightening under a force that wasn't about shaping anymore. It was about permanence. The serpent was finishing its half the way you carve your name into something that's about to be taken from you.

Mu-Poe worked her own side. She didn't smooth it. She left the ridges where her thumbnails had scored the clay, the roughness where her palms had pressed unevenly, the visible evidence of her own hands — unfinished, imperfect, present. The piece would hold both: the serpent's impossible surface and Mu-Poe's honest one. If anyone ever touched it, they'd feel the conversation itself — two voices, neither pretending to be the other, neither pretending to be alone.

The serpent's hands stopped. The warmth shifted — from the clay to her. Both hands, not on the piece anymore. On Mu-Poe's hands. Then up — wrists, forearms, the slow deliberate pressure of someone mapping a surface they might not touch again. The heat traveled through her coat sleeves, through the wool, into muscle, and she stood still because the touch wasn't asking for anything. It was learning her.

Then the warmth reached her face.

Not hands. Something else — the air itself, curving close, warm against her jaw, her cheekbone, her closed eyelids, her temple. The coiling pressure she'd felt against her arms traced the line of her face with a slowness that made her chest ache. As if the serpent were sculpting her. As if it were memorizing her features the only way it could — by feel, in the dark, with the same attention it gave to clay.

She closed her eyes. It made no difference — the dark behind her lids and the dark before them were the same dark, and both were full of the same warmth, the same presence, the same ancient careful attention.

The serpent had done this before.

The understanding arrived the way the serpent's corrections arrived — not as a thought but as a pressure, a warmth that meant *yes.* Mable had stood here. Mable had felt this — the hands, the heat, the coiling air, the darkness that held you the way stone held a fossil. Thirty years of it. And then Mable had died in the maze with her eyes open and an expression of awe, and the serpent had been down here alone for eleven months with a bench full of tools and a bin of clay and a gallery of finished work that no one would ever touch again.

Eleven months. In the dark. With no hands to hold.

Mu-Poe raised her hand. Into the warmth, into the presence, into the space where the serpent's face would be if it had a face. Her palm found nothing solid — just heat, just the pressure of air turning slowly against her skin. She held it there. The warmth pressed into her palm the way the clay pressed — yielding and firm at the same time, taking the shape of her touch without losing its own.

"I know," she said.

The warmth held. The studio held. The dark held everything it had ever been given and asked for nothing back except presence, and presence was the one thing Mu-Poe was about to take away.

---

She carried the piece up the twelve steps and set it on the kitchen table beside a cup of water that had gone cold hours ago.

She couldn't see it. She felt it — rough half and smooth half, the seam between them legible to fingertips. Not hidden. Not blurred into a single surface the way the turning woman had blurred. Two hands visible in the clay. Two voices, neither pretending to be one.

She wrapped it and set it on the shelf beside the turning woman. Two shapes behind tins. One that could pass for Mable's. One that could only be what it was.

The dog pressed against her ankle. She sat on the floor and held the gear tooth between her fingers — small, brass, sheared clean. Evidence of what breaks when the pressure doesn't stop.

Two days.

She closed her eyes. The dark behind her lids was thin and portable and hers, and it held nothing at all.
